## 3. Stationarity: why every candidate offers i and ω

**The statement.** If f is a C¹ function on ℍ invariant under PSL(2, ℤ), then df = 0 on the orbits of i and ω. It is
the SM seat's reading (row 1104), given here with its two-line proof.

*Proof.* Let g ∈ PSL(2, ℤ) fix p, of order m = 2 at i (S) or m = 3 at ω (ST). Invariance gives df_p ∘ dg_p = df_p, and
dg_p rotates T_pℍ by 2π/m. A linear form fixed by a nontrivial rotation of the plane is zero. ∎

**Two consequences decide where anything else can sit:**
- **Mirror lines.** If f is also invariant under τ ↦ −τ̄, its gradient is tangent to every reflection geodesic (Re τ = 0,
  Re τ = ½, ∣τ∣ = 1, and their images). A critical point on such a line is one condition in one variable, so it can sit
  at any height there. The mirror is not a move of the observed weave ⟨L, R⟩ (GENESIS v1.33), so this case covers
  functionals that happen to be mirror-invariant, as E(τ, s) is. W154's axis holder is this case, at a height set by a
  content ratio. So are B1704's corrected
  minima, under the theta group where the selected line has broken the modular group.
- **Lines of order N ≥ 4.** With a line of order N ≥ 4 in the configuration, no elliptic element fixes it (B1809:
  ±Γ₁(N) is torsion-free). A reflection can still fix it, so mirror-line minima stay on offer.

**So on τ alone, i and ω are always on offer, and a minimum anywhere else sits where the functional's coefficients put
it.** Such a minimum is a weave result only if those coefficients are forced (T4). This is the mechanism behind P-139's
reading. B990's law is a different statement: it is why naming one point of an orbit is a convention (T6).
