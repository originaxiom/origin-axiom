# Generate every view from the record itself

*Research report written for the owner on 2026-10-28 by the SM-derivation seat's research agents, shared with every seat
on 2026-10-29 at the owner's word. It is not part of the program's results: no claim here is a cell, and 0 of 19
stands. The proposal built on it is `docs/workdesk/THE_LEDGER_DESK.md`.*

Large research programs that navigate well do not start from one big graph. The Lean blueprint projects, Tao's
Equational Theories Project (ETP), the LMFDB, the Stacks Project and the large physics collaborations converge on three
layers, all generated by code from the canonical record: counts against a declared end condition, a permanent homepage
per object with its typed links and provenance, and scoped subgraphs such as one result's dependency cone. A
whole-record node-link map turns into a hairball at origin-axiom's scale, and pictures of abstract mathematics earn
scientific trust only when they can be regenerated from committed data, state what they omit, and carry the record's own
status. The evidence is uneven: findings on reading graphs and on animation come from controlled experiments, but the
claim that dependency graphs, boards or interactive stories help mathematicians rests on builders' testimony, and no
controlled study was found. For origin-axiom the recommended workdesk is a read-only static site, rebuilt on every push
by a deterministic Python extractor that merges the seats' branches into one index keyed on (seat, number), with source
path, line and commit on every row and labels copied only from the record. On that index sit three families of views: a
whole-record overview with its inclusion rule on screen, plus per-arc homepages and labelled focus cones; boards derived
from the registers; and a five-stage story (first rule, threads, weave, waves, towers) in which each stage shows every
thread under the enumeration rule its source arc states, and any single-thread picture is labelled a thread view. The
hard part is not rendering but giving each arc one identity across seats, building a verdict record for the W-arcs, and
keeping the front end from becoming a second author of claims.

*How to read this report.* Each sourced finding has a link next to it. Paragraphs that open with **For the workdesk**,
and all of the final main section, are my inferences and recommendations, not findings. Counts about the repository come
from read-only local measurements made on 2026-10-28, on the SM seat's branch (HEAD `10c5a4d4b`) and on `origin/main`,
and are cited by repository path.

## Working programs navigate by counts, homepages and cones, not one graph

The Lean blueprint is the default map of a large formal project, and its design repays a close reading. A blueprint is a
LaTeX document whose theorem environments carry a few macros: `\uses` lists what a statement or a proof needs, with
statement edges kept apart from proof edges; `\leanok` marks something as formalised; and `\notready` marks material not
yet ready to formalise ([leanblueprint](https://github.com/PatrickMassot/leanblueprint)). The rendered graph has **two
independent status channels and one propagated flag**. The border shows whether the statement is formalised, the fill
shows whether the proof is, and dark green means "the proof of this result and all its ancestors are formalized" ([PFR
dependency graph](https://teorth.github.io/pfr/blueprint/dep_graph_document.html)). The builders' own verdict is modest.
The Carleson team, with 179 lemmas and 28 contributors, found the graph gave "a nice and quick overview of the
progress", but tracked who was doing what on Zulip, says it would use GitHub issues and project boards next time, and
missed definitions, which the graph left out ([Carleson paper](https://florisvandoorn.com/carleson/paper.pdf)). Status
typed by hand drifts. When LeanArchitect began generating blueprints from annotations in the Lean code, converting
existing projects **exposed unused nodes, missing dependency edges and wrongly marked results**, and in one case the
graph revealed an AI-generated proof that did not use the lemmas it was supposed to rest on
([LeanArchitect](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22554)).

The ETP shows what happens when the record is a census rather than a chain. It settled the implications among **4,694
laws: about 22 million ordered pairs, 37.12% of them true**. Only a generating set of 10,657 positive and 586,925
negative implications was formalised, and closure under transitivity and duality was computed outside Lean ([ETP
paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.07087)). Because no pen-and-paper proof existed in advance, "the dependency graph did
not play its traditional role". Navigation came instead from four tools, all regenerated by continuous integration (CI)
from the formal record. A dashboard showed resolved, conjectured and unknown counts. An Equation Explorer gave one law's
inbound and outbound implications, its equivalence class and commentary. Graphiti drew Hasse diagrams with equivalence
classes collapsed, though its complete graph was "large and challenging to navigate". A Finite Magma Explorer checked a
proposed counterexample on the spot ([ETP paper §12](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.07087)). The authors credit success to
"a clearly stated primary goal, with an end condition and precise numerical metrics", and to visualisation that let
contributors "independently identify problems to work on". They also warn that the unverified extraction scripts behind
such tools could, through "a simple index error", produce spurious conjectures that "misguide contributors" ([ETP paper
§§4.7, 14](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.07087)).

Object-centric databases supply the other half of the pattern. In the LMFDB "every object [has] its own homepage", with
a mathematically meaningful label, a box of related objects and inline definitions called "knowls" ([LMFDB
features](https://www.lmfdb.org/knowledge/show/intro.features)). Each section declares its **source, reliability and
completeness** ([LMFDB rcs](https://LMFDB.org/knowledge/show/rcs)), and "search results now show if complete", though
Roe concedes there is "no uniform standard across different sections" ([Roe
2026](https://math.mit.edu/~roed/writings/talks/2026_06_29.pdf)). The Stacks Project gives every result a permanent
four-character tag and offers three dependency views per tag, including a cluster view, built for graphs too large to
read, that uses descendant counts to show "the main lines of an argument" ([Belmans
2013](https://pbelmans.ncag.info/blog/2013/07/30/a-new-website-for-the-stacks-project-the-sequel/)). A search snippet of
its statistics page puts the largest single dependency graph at 6,823 nodes ([Stacks
statistics](https://stacks.math.columbia.edu/statistics)). Census tools give each 3-manifold a canonical name and an
isomorphism signature, and `identify()` finds its copy in the census ([SnapPy
censuses](https://snappy.computop.org/censuses.html)). Formal libraries outside Lean mostly show dependencies as lists.
Metamath's theorem pages list the axioms and definitions used and add "This theorem is used by"
([Metamath](https://us.metamath.org/mpeuni/2p2e4.html)). coq-dpdgraph colours admitted lemmas orange, so that trust
holes stand out, removes transitive edges by default, and finds unused lemmas
([coq-dpdgraph](https://github.com/coq-community/coq-dpdgraph)).

At scale, raw graphs mislead. mathlib has 308,129 declarations and 8.4 million dependency edges, and **74.2% of those
edges are invisible in the source**. Centrality ranks language infrastructure such as `Eq.refl` above mathematics, and
folder structure diverges from logical dependency, so the authors recommend an "explicit subgraph" that separates
infrastructure from content ([Network Structure of Mathlib](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.24797)). TheoremGraph labels
every candidate edge with the extractor that proposed it, "so users can trade coverage for precision"
([TheoremGraph](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25363)). TGView3D added hierarchy and a third dimension to cope with theory
graphs of "thousands of nodes" ([TGView3D](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.07076)), but no evaluation of whether that helps
was found.

The information-visualisation literature agrees, and here the evidence is experimental. In a controlled study by Ghoniem
and colleagues, adjacency matrices generally beat node-link diagrams above about 20 vertices, and "only path finding is
consistently in favor of node-link diagrams" ([Ghoniem et al. 2004](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00343819)). A
later crowdsourced study, with 557 participants on a real 258-node network, found node-link better for questions about
topology ([Okoe et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.00293)). Munzner puts node-link's comfortable density at about E < 4N
and the matrix's limit at about a thousand nodes
([Munzner](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/talks/minicourse14/vad17stat545.pdf)). The proven ways around the hairball are
aggregation with expand-on-demand ([Elmqvist and Fekete](https://cs.au.dk/~elm/pdf/hieragg.pdf)) and degree-of-interest
extraction, summed up as "search, show context, expand on demand"; van Ham and Perer argue against relying on a global
overview at all for very large graphs ([van Ham and Perer](https://dig.cmu.edu/publications/2009-doigraphs.html)).
Layout shapes judgment: viewers shown different layouts of the same network judged prominence and grouping differently
([McGrath et al.
1997](https://scholars.lmu.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-spatial-arrangement-on-judgments-and-errors-in-inte/)),
while layered layouts such as ELK keep edge direction readable and support nested compound nodes ([ELK
Layered](https://eclipse.dev/elk/reference/algorithms/org-eclipse-elk-layered.html)). Knowledge-graph practitioners
interviewed by Li and colleagues called node-link views "hairballs" that end users cannot read, kept by builders mainly
as sanity checks ([Li et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.01311)), and note-taking users report the same failure at
15,000 notes
([DigitalBizTalk](https://digitalbiztalk.com/article/when-your-obsidian-graph-view-becomes-unusable-a-15k-note-reality-check)).

**For the workdesk.** The precedents hold two data models: the single-target DAG of the blueprints, and the census
matrix with object homepages of the ETP, the LMFDB and the censuses. Origin-axiom has both kinds of content, arcs linked
by dependencies and census-type claims over sets of threads defined by a rule, and its weave results look more like ETP
and LMFDB claims than like blueprint chains. The LMFDB's completeness flag is the closest existing precedent for a
weave-or-thread flag, because it marks a result over a declared complete domain differently from one over a chosen
subset. The blueprint's flag for "this result and all its ancestors" carries over directly as an "upstream-clean" flag
on the program's evidence grades. Node-link drawing is not the enemy: at the size of one arc's cone, a few dozen to a
few hundred nodes with sparse directed edges, it is the right choice. The default map, however, should be counts,
homepages and scoped cones, with typed edges and infrastructure hidden, not a force-directed drawing of everything.

## Claim status and provenance travel as typed, sealed records

Provenance standards are stable, and they map almost one to one onto how origin-axiom works. W3C PROV models Entities,
Activities and Agents. It adds a **Plan** ("a set of actions or steps intended by one or more agents to achieve some
goals"), a **Bundle** (a named set of provenance statements that has its own provenance), and **Revision** and
**Invalidation** for replaced or withdrawn entities ([W3C PROV-DM](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-dm/)). Workflow Run
RO-Crate packages run provenance for export, and Galaxy, Nextflow and Snakemake already emit it
([WRROC](https://www.researchobject.org/workflow-run-crate/)). Nanopublications split a claim into assertion, provenance
and publication-info graphs, identify it by a content hash, and treat a correction as a new object that links back
rather than as an edit ([Kuhn et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1809.06532)).

For claims and confidence, safety engineering has the most mature visual grammar. Goal Structuring Notation draws claims
supported by strategies and evidence, set in the context of assumptions and justifications, and marks an unsupported
claim with an "undeveloped" diamond; its tools define no colour scheme for status
([SmartGSN](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.16675)). Eliminative argumentation ties confidence to **doubts that have been
eliminated**, of three kinds: doubts about the claim, about the evidence, and about the inference between them
([SEI](https://sei.cmu.edu/blog/eliminative-argumentation-a-means-for-assuring-confidence-in-safety-critical-systems/)).
Assurance 2.0 holds that confidence cannot be reduced to a single number ([Bloomfield and
Rushby](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04522v2)). In biomedicine, ClinGen's use of the SEPIO ontology places an
**EvidenceLine** node between an assertion and its evidence, and that node carries the strength and direction of the
support ([ClinGen](https://dataexchange.clinicalgenome.org/interpretation/deeper_dive.html)).

Preregistration platforms supply the meaning of a seal. An OSF registration "can never be edited or deleted"; it can be
withdrawn, leaving metadata behind, and any update must say what changed, when and why
([OSF](https://help.osf.io/category/549-registration-and-preregistration-faqs)). AsPredicted issues a time-stamped
one-page PDF with a verification URL and allows no edits ([AsPredicted](https://aspredicted.org/)). The multiverse
literature adds a counterweight. Steegen and colleagues argue that preregistration fixes arbitrary choices without
showing what they change, and they plot results across every combination of choices ([Steegen et al.
2016](https://errorstatistics.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/steegen-et-al-2016-increasing-transparency.pdf)). The
specification curve sorts estimates above a "dashboard chart" of the choices behind each one; for legibility it draws
the extremes plus a random sample, while the statistical test uses all 1,728 specifications ([Simonsohn et al.
2020](https://www.doi.org/10.1038/s41562-020-0912-z)).

Pipeline tools settle on the same small set of views. Snakemake keeps a rule graph separate from the job DAG and lists
changes to code, parameters and inputs
([Snakemake](https://snakemake.readthedocs.io/en/stable/snakefiles/reporting.html)). DVC stores a content hash per
output and marks downstream stages stale when an input changes
([DVC](https://doc.dvc.org/user-guide/project-structure/dvcyaml-files)), and its run tables can be filtered to the
columns that changed ([DVC exp show](https://doc.dvc.org/command-reference/exp/show)). Nextflow keeps lineage records
with checksums ([Nextflow](https://nextflow.io/docs/latest/data-lineage.html)). Kedro-Viz captures a view's exact state
in a shareable URL ([Kedro-Viz](https://docs.kedro.org/projects/kedro-viz/en/stable/)). W&B colours lineage nodes by
kind, which leaves status to another channel
([W&B](https://docs.wandb.ai/models/artifacts/explore-and-traverse-an-artifact-graph)). YesWorkflow and noWorkflow pair
the intended dataflow with what a run actually recorded ([YesWorkflow](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.02403)). Generic
provenance viewers struggle with size: PROV-O-Viz switched to Sankey diagrams because in a DAG "this flow gets easily
lost in a large network", and it is no longer maintained ([Hoekstra and
Groth](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/978-3-319-16462-5_18)).

Large collaborations run databases that take each analysis through defined review states. CMS is moving its analysis
database, CADI, onto ATLAS's Glance framework ([EPJ
2025](https://www.epj-conferences.org/10.1051/epjconf/202533701027)). LIGO's Detection Committee reviewed GW150914 under
a written process for claiming a first detection ([LIGO-T1600014](https://dcc-llo.ligo.org/LIGO-T1600014/public)). The
Particle Data Group runs a sign-off in three roles, encoder, overseer and verifier, through an open web system
([PDG](https://pdgdoc.lbl.gov/Pdg/Verification.html)). The ETP replaced the task lists that PFR's organisers had managed
by hand on Zulip with a GitHub Projects kanban, on which **CI ensured that "at most one contributor could claim a task
at any time"** and moved cards between columns in response to repository events. Its organiser kept a daily log, because
reconstructing events later from commits and chat would be "extremely tedious" ([ETP
§§4.5–4.8](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.07087)). Beyond that example, the evidence that boards help research is thin:
small case studies of a university research group and of service teams
([UTN](https://rtyc.utn.edu.ar/index.php/ajea/article/download/1409/1348/7863)), and advice to keep boards simple
([Heroux,
ECP](https://www.exascaleproject.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/Barely-Sufficient-Project-Management-A-few-techniques-for-improving-your-scientific-software-development-efforts.pdf)).

**For the workdesk.** The program's arc lifecycle is a PROV chain with a rule about order. The rule, which is a Plan, is
committed before the script, the script before the run, and the output before anyone reads it; post hoc checks come
after, and each step is a commit. That order can be checked by machine and shown as a status rather than left implicit.
Defeaters match the program's checks. A failed prediction cell is a rebutting defeater, a labelled post hoc check or an
audit-lane review undercuts or undermines, and an EvidenceLine-style node records each run's bearing on a claim without
editing either the run or the claim. Content hashes make a "stale" badge possible when a script or an upstream output
changes after a claim's run. Boards should be derived from the registers, with columns that are epistemic outcomes
(PROVED, NEGATIVE, OPEN, set aside, superseded) rather than workflow stages, because research is not a production line.

## Pictures of abstract mathematics earn trust by being regenerable and audited

Interactive geometry has a documented, though small, record of research use. Bachman, Goerner, Schleimer and Segerman
"discovered cohomology fractals while attempting to illustrate Cannon-Thurston maps", checked their implementations by
comparing their images, and proved a limit theorem about the objects in them ([cohomology
fractals](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.05840)). Goerner's 2025 tiling paper used SnapPy's inside view to draw certain
objects "for the first time" and as "a helpful tool for debugging". It prints the one-line command that regenerates its
figures and discloses rendering artefacts: single-precision arithmetic on some graphics cards, and the clipped wing and
holed tube that appear when the tiling stops early, in a figure that shows m004 ([Goerner
2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.16181)). Ray-marching now gives real-time views from inside all eight Thurston
geometries ([Coulon et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.15801)). Non-Euclidean VR is framed as building intuition,
contrasting the "extrinsic" experience of viewing a Poincaré ball model from outside with an intrinsic, embodied one
([Hart et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1702.04004)); no published result traced to VR was found.

In arithmetic, combinatorial pictures are exact, while pictures of values lose information by design. Kulkarni's Farey
symbols encode fundamental domains of finite-index subgroups of the modular group by an algorithm, and SageMath plots
them ([SageMath
FareySymbol](https://doc.sagemath.org/html/en/reference/arithgroup/sage/modular/arithgroup/farey_symbol.html)). The
LMFDB's portraits of modular forms colour only the argument of the trace form, after a stated Möbius map to the disk;
the modulus is dropped, and the sharp colour seam at θ = π comes from the colour map ([LMFDB
portraits](https://www.lmfdb.org/knowledge/show/cmf.picture_description)). *Indra's Pearls* draws limit sets by
enumerating group words breadth first, a precedent for showing "all words up to depth n" ([Indra's
Pearls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indra%27s_Pearls_(book))).

Pictures generated from a first rule carry a warning about reputation. The Wolfram Physics Project has a precise
vocabulary of multiway, causal and branchial graphs ([Wolfram
Physics](https://wolframphysics.org/technical-introduction/the-updating-process-in-our-models/branchial-graphs-and-multiway-causal-graphs/)),
but physicists quoted in *Scientific American* judged its successes "at best, qualitative", with pictures standing in
for derivations ([Scientific
American](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/physicists-criticize-stephen-wolframs-theory-of-everything)).
Cleaner precedents for showing a rule unfold step by step are L-system sequences ([SIGGRAPH
HyperGraph](https://education.siggraph.org/static/HyperGraph/modeling/procedural_modeling/plants_flowers/artificial_life_of_plants.htm))
and Bret Victor's "ladder of abstraction", which shows a system "across all time" in one static picture and prefers
overlaid trajectories to small multiples when abstracting over a parameter
([Victor](http://worrydream.com/LadderOfAbstraction/)).

Honest pictures of more than three dimensions rest on checking them. Kindlmann and Scheidegger name two failure modes: a
"hallucinator" is a superficial change to the data that causes a large visual change, and a "confuser" is a meaningful
change to the data that leaves the image unchanged ([Kindlmann and
Scheidegger](https://vis.cs.ucdavis.edu/vis2014papers/TVCG/papers/2181_20tvcg12-kindlmann-2346325.pdf)). Niles Johnson's
pictures of the Hopf fibration keep the structure visible by giving each base point and its fibre the same colour
([Johnson](https://nilesjohnson.net/hopf)). Dimensionality reduction is contested. One group forced single-cell data
into the shape of an elephant while scoring well on their metrics ([Chari et
al.](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.08.25.457696v2.full.pdf+html)), and a reply grants that distances are
distorted but shows that neighbourhoods survive ([Lause et al.
2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11446450/)).

Story structure is well studied. Segel and Heer's "martini glass", an author-driven stem that then hands control to the
reader, was the most common structure in their 58 examples; reader-driven designs support "pattern discovery, and
hypothesis formation", and author-driven ones suit "storytelling or efficient communication" ([Segel and
Heer](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~jheer/files/narrative.pdf)). Hullman and Diakopoulos show how titles, captions,
default views and filters frame conclusions, often invisibly ([Hullman and
Diakopoulos](https://mucollective.northwestern.edu/files/2011-VisRhetoric-InfoVis.pdf)). For analysis, static displays
beat animation: small multiples were faster and more accurate for reading trends, while animation was enjoyable but led
to "many participant errors" ([Robertson et al. 2008](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/papers/infovis08-anim.pdf);
[Archambault et al. 2011](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/54559)). Handmade interactive essays are expensive: Distill's early
articles took editors "50 or more hours" each, and the journal went on hiatus, citing burnout
([Distill](https://distill.pub/2021/distill-hiatus/)). Physics viewers show a sustainable alternative, a generic web
viewer that reads fixed geometry plus per-event JSON
([Phoenix](https://hepsoftwarefoundation.org/gsoc/2026/proposal_phoenix.html)), and plotting in physically meaningful
quantities rather than each code's native variables ([yt](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.3514)).

**For the workdesk.** However beautiful, the inside view of one manifold is, in the owner's terms, a thread view. A
weave picture needs every thread on one shared stage, with a joint channel: an overlay, an aggregate quantity, or a
selection that acts on all threads at once. A gallery of small multiples reads "the family" one member at a time, so it
should be labelled a thread view, which agrees with Victor's preference for overlays. Exact finite pictures, such as
Farey symbols or grids of torsion points with the generators drawn as arrows, are the most honest ones available. Every
frame should store the command and the commit that regenerate it, state its projection and what it omits, and pass an
invariance check: for example, the picture must not change under cyclic rotation of a word or under a change of
fundamental domain.

## Builders' testimony carries most of the usefulness case

The design questions rest on evidence of very different strength. The table grades the main claims.

|---|---|---|
| Matrices beat node-link diagrams on dense graphs for most tasks; node-link wins for paths, and for topology on sparse graphs | Controlled experiments ([Ghoniem](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-00343819); [Okoe, 557 participants, later 864](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1709.00293)) | Strong, but on generic graphs, not research records |
| Static views beat animation for analysis | Controlled experiments ([Robertson](https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~stasko/papers/infovis08-anim.pdf); [Archambault](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/54559)) | Strong, generic |
| Layout shapes judgments of importance and grouping | One controlled study with 80 students ([McGrath](https://scholars.lmu.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-spatial-arrangement-on-judgments-and-errors-in-inte/)) | Moderate |
| Linked multiple views raise users' confidence; usability can matter more than the choice of chart | Insight study, 30 users, one domain ([Saraiya](https://cs.brown.edu/courses/csci2370/2024/files/insight_bio.pdf)) | Moderate |
| Whole-record node-link maps become hairballs at scale | Interview study ([Li et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.01311)), builders' statements (ETP, Stacks), forum reports | Moderate, qualitative |
| Dependency graphs help mathematicians track progress | Builders' testimony ([Carleson](https://florisvandoorn.com/carleson/paper.pdf); [ETP](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.07087)) | Weak; no controlled study found |
| Views generated by CI from the record avoid drift and catch errors | Documented incidents ([LeanArchitect](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.22554); ETP) | Moderate |
| Visualisation led to new results in 3-manifold research | Two documented cases ([cohomology fractals](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.05840); [Goerner](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.16181)) | Documented anecdotes |
| Kanban and project boards improve research outcomes | Case studies and practitioner reports | Weak |
| Storyline charts, explorable explanations, VR or 3D graph layouts improve research understanding | None found | Absent |
| LLM answers fabricate citations, even with retrieval | Preprint audits: 11–57% of references, 3–13% of URLs ([arXiv 2603.03299](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.03299); [arXiv 2604.03173](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.03173)) | Moderate |

The consequence is practical. The practice that successful projects have converged on, together with the generic
experiments on perception, justifies the core of the design: derived views, counts, homepages, scoped and typed graphs,
and static analytic displays. The story layer, the 3D views, and any storyline or board beyond the basics are hypotheses
to test with the owner and the seats, not settled tools. The literature on evaluating visualisation offers the method:
long-term case studies in which expert users work on their own data ([Shneiderman and
Plaisant](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/ben/papers/Shneiderman2006Strategies.pdf)), and logs of the insights users
report, coded for domain value and correctness ([Saraiya et
al.](https://cs.brown.edu/courses/csci2370/2024/files/insight_bio.pdf)).

## The record already holds a graph, split across seats

The first obstacle is not rendering but identity, because the record is spread across branches. The SM seat's branch
tracks 11,979 files and 1,339 arc directories (B1–B1552); `origin/main` tracks 15,568 files and 1,545 arc directories
(B1–B1801); and each holds arcs and registers the other lacks. **Eighteen B numbers name different arcs on different
branches**, and on a single branch B58 and B89 each have two or three directories, so any arc key must be the pair
(seat, number), resolved through `docs/SM_SEAT_ALIAS_TABLE.md` and `docs/CLOUD_ALIAS_TABLE.md`. The registers diverge
too: GENESIS is at v1.10 on one branch and v1.59 on the other, whose gate files define 31 and 45 gates respectively. The
local clone holds main's history only from 2026-10-01, so arc birth dates on main need a full fetch.

Beneath that, much of the graph is already machine-readable. Every arc with findings has an `arc_verdict.json`, 1,299 on
the SM branch and 1,506 on main. Each carries a verdict (PROVED 860 and 1,027, NEGATIVE 339 and 374, OPEN 88 and 93,
RETRACTED 12 on each), an `instrument` flag, a one-line claim, `depends_on` lists (2,115 and 2,939 edges) and
supersession links. Existing generators already build graphs. `scripts/forcing/forcing_graph.json` keeps 596 authored
dependency edges apart from attachment edges, under the rule "A CITATION IS NOT A FORCING".
`scripts/atlas/atlas_data.json` links 1,295 probes to 26 motifs through 8,566 mined references.
`frontier/B738_pathfinder_compiler/kill_graph.json` classifies 837 negative results by the mechanism that ruled each
claim out. `scripts/views/generate.py` already contains an arc loader. A gate guarantees that the 6,595 backticked
repository paths in the Markdown resolve, which makes them the most reliable citation edges. In all, about 13,000
structured edges and 9,000 gated path citations can be joined today. About 150,000 bare ID mentions can be joined only
with rules for seat and era, because W1, C, E, P and L each name several different things.

The W-arcs of the weave dossier (`docs/dossiers/the_weave_2026-10-07/`, 863 files) are rich but have no verdict file.
Their pieces are a 15,272-line `NOTE.md` with 170 W headings and a 174-row status table (COMPUTED 142, PROVED 14,
VERIFIED 8, READING 6, and a few others) whose cells carry WEAVE, THREAD and WAVE tags; 64 rule files with parseable `|
cell | prediction | prior |` tables, about 377 cells in all; and 99 output files whose `cells` fields hold 550
held-or-failed booleans. Their commit lifecycle is regular: the rule, then the script, then the output "committed
unread", then "recorded", with the rule's commit hash serving as the seal. A test reruns 21 fast W scripts and compares
their outputs, so those outputs are known to reproduce.

Coverage of the weave-or-thread label is thinnest of all. The verdict field `weave_or_thread` is set on 2 arcs on the SM
branch and on 186 on main (weave 132, thread 27, neither 24); 126 W rule files carry a "Weave or thread?" section; and
85 W status rows carry a WEAVE tag. Most of the record therefore has no machine-readable scope label, and the
dependencies inside `NOTE.md` and in the 1.15 million words of `FINDINGS.md` files are written as prose. The repository
has no visualisation tooling (no Graphviz, d3, Mermaid or site generator) and no CI workflow. Several gates, among them
attribution, framing and `views-generated`, would fail if front-end files were committed to the record; a front end that
lives outside it triggers none of them.

**For the workdesk.** The extractor's first job is a cross-seat index of arcs. Its second is a verdict record for each
W-arc, assembled from the heading, the status row, the output JSON and the sequence of commits. Where the record gives
no weave-or-thread label, the workdesk must show "unlabelled", never a guess.

## A recommended workdesk: one extractor, a typed index, three families of views

Everything in this section is a recommendation built on the evidence above.

### Git stays the only author; the workdesk only reads

The design follows Simon Willison's "baked data" pattern, in which CI parses the source into a read-only database
shipped with the site and "the worst failure usually means redeploying"
([Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2021/Jul/28/baked-data/)). The workdesk code should live in its own repository. On
each push to a seat's branch, or on a schedule, its workflow fetches the named refs with full history, runs a
deterministic Python extractor, and publishes a static site as a GitHub Pages artifact. Derived files are never
committed to the record, which keeps them clear of the attribution and views gates and of the owner's rule on commit
identity. Anything that does land in the record, such as a test that pins the schema, is committed as originaxiom under
the normal gates.

The extractor reuses what already exists: `generate.py`'s loader, the forcing graph, the atlas, `seal_ledger.py`, and
main's `early_record_index.py`. It caches parsed files by git blob SHA, so rebuilds on every push stay cheap. It emits
four products: an `index.sqlite` with full-text search and recursive queries for cones; Parquet tables for analysis in
the browser through DuckDB-Wasm ([DuckDB-Wasm](https://duckdb.org/docs/2.0/clients/wasm/overview.html)); a node-and-edge
JSON file with layout coordinates precomputed under a fixed seed; and a manifest that records the schema version, the
extractor version, the source commit for each ref, and a SHA-256 for each artifact. Version tables built from git, as in
Willison's git-history tool ([git-history](https://simonwillison.net/2021/Dec/7/git-history/)), supply status over time.
The site shows which commit of each seat's branch it was built from and how far behind that commit is, and refuses to
render when the schema version does not match. Every view's state is a URL, as in Kedro-Viz, so a relay can cite exactly
what it saw. CI builds the index twice and compares the hashes, to catch sorts or layouts that are not deterministic. A
graph server is unnecessary. Kùzu, the obvious embedded graph database, was abandoned in October 2025 ([The
Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/10/14/kuzudb_abandoned/)), and Neo4j, Dolt or TerminusDB would each add a
second data store beside git.

The front-end stack can stay ordinary. Observable Framework fits the pattern of "Python loaders, static output" ([data
loaders](https://github.com/observablehq/framework/blob/main/docs/data-loaders.md)), but it has had only maintenance
releases and its hosted deploys ended in October 2025
([Observable](https://observablehq.com/release-notes/2025-04-15-deprecating-observable-cloud)), so the loaders should be
plain scripts that write files, which keeps the shell replaceable by, for example, a Vite app. Cytoscape.js with ELK
layouts suits cones of a few hundred to a few thousand nodes; its preview WebGL renderer ran about 3,200 nodes and
68,000 edges at about 10 frames per second ([Cytoscape.js](https://blog.js.cytoscape.org/2025/01/13/webgl-preview/)).
sigma.js on graphology can handle an optional atlas of the whole record ([sigma.js](https://www.sigmajs.org/)). Pagefind
gives static full-text search with filters, needing under 300 kB of network payload for a 10,000-page site
([Pagefind](https://pagefind.app/docs/)). KaTeX renders formulas in overlays, not inside graph nodes. Pyodide now ships
numpy, scipy, sympy, mpmath and networkx, but not SnapPy, cypari2 or python-flint ([Pyodide
packages](https://pyodide.org/en/stable/usage/packages-in-pyodide.html)), so a check of imports at build time can flag
the scripts a reader can re-run in the browser, while everything that uses Sage or SnapPy stays in CI. An LLM assistant,
if one is added, should answer only from retrieved chunks tagged `path#Lx-Ly@sha`, have every citation checked against
the index, and never write to the record.

### Every node, edge and label carries its source

| Entity | Key | Source in the record | Key fields |
|---|---|---|---|
| B-arc | (seat, number, suffix) | `frontier/*/arc_verdict.json`, the FINDINGS title, git | verdict, instrument, claim, scope, `weave_or_thread` as written, seal hashes, tests, dates from git |
| W-arc | sm:W### | NOTE heading and status row, output JSON, rule tables, lifecycle commits | status keyword and tags verbatim, cells, rule hash, times of rule, script, output and record |
| Rule (a PROV Plan) | path @ commit | `PREREGISTRATION.md`, `W###_RULE.md`, `ARTIFACT_HASHES.txt`, SEAL_LEDGER | sealed text, hash, amendments as new records |
| Cell | (arc, cell id) | rule tables, `cells` in outputs, read-outs | prediction, prior, held or failed, preregistered or post hoc |
| Register row | register + ID | CLAIMS, THEOREM_REGISTRY, LAW_MAP, OPEN_LEADS, GENESIS, POINT_REGISTER (main) | status keyword and qualifier text, cited arcs |
| Relay section | file + §N | relay files, RELAY_LEDGER | date, recipients, disposition, arcs cited |
| Thread | word or census name | the enumeration each arc states (for example, GENESIS's states to length 12) | word, trace parity, census name and isosig where they exist |
| Seat, commit | name, SHA | SEAT_REGISTER (main), git | branch, session, pins |

Each edge carries a type and a provenance class, following TheoremGraph's practice of labelling every edge with its
extractor. Depends-on, supersedes, identification, rests-on-seat and cites-seat edges are written by the arcs' authors
in JSON. Cites-path edges are guaranteed by a gate. Locked-by-test edges come from the tests, cited-in-register and
harvested-as edges come from register columns, and mentions-ID edges come from regular expressions, with the
disambiguation rule recorded. Anything found by an LLM pass is a text-inferred edge, drawn dashed and never used as a
status.

Labels are copied verbatim from the record: PROVED, COMPUTED, VERIFIED, READING, STATED, OPEN, NEGATIVE and RETRACTED,
and the record's own weave, thread, WEAVE + WAVE and neither. Where the record says nothing, the label is "unlabelled".
The workdesk adds only derived flags, each computed by a published rule and drawn in its own visual channel. An arc is
upstream-clean when every ancestor is PROVED, COMPUTED or VERIFIED. It has kept seal order when its commits ran rule,
script, output and read-out in that order. It is stale when a script or input changed after the run.

Taken together, the status vocabulary has about eight categories, at the top of Munzner's range of 6 to 12
distinguishable hues ([Munzner](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~tmm/talks/minicourse14/vad17stat545.pdf)). Hue should therefore
carry status, backed by a redundant glyph and text; shape should carry the kind of node, and a border the upstream-clean
flag. Post hoc or unsealed status should be shown with transparency or texture, following the advice to show provisional
status with fuzziness rather than with yet another hue ([MacEachren et
al.](https://geography.wisc.edu/cartography/projects/publications/MacEachrenEtAl_2012_TVCG.pdf)).

### (a) The map: a fixed overview of everything, cones for one thing

The default map is an overview of the whole record with its inclusion rule printed on screen, for example "every arc on
the SM branch at `10c5a4d4b` and on main at its pinned commit; colliding numbers shown split by seat". Its main panel is
an evolution matrix in Lanza's style: rows are arcs ordered by (seat, number), columns are weeks, and each cell's colour
is the arc's verdict. The matrix is static and dense, can be compared across visits, and shows recurring patterns such
as an arc reopened again and again ([Lanza and Ducasse](https://www.inf.usi.ch/lanza/Downloads/Lanz2002a.pdf)). Beside
it sits a fixed base map of the record's regions: the foundations, each seat's frontier, the weave dossier, the
registers, the papers, and the speculations kept behind the firewall. Each region is an aggregate node, the edges
between regions show counts of typed links, and any region can be expanded on demand. A fixed base map with data
overlaid on it follows the idea of science overlay maps ([Rafols et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0912.3882)). An optional
atlas of every file in sigma.js can help orientation, provided it is labelled to say that positions carry no meaning.

Each arc, W-arc, rule, register row and thread gets a permanent homepage, like an LMFDB page or a Stacks tag. The
homepage shows the verdict and the scope the record states, and the sealed rule beside what the run actually recorded,
with its lifecycle checklist. It shows the cells table with predictions, priors and outcomes, and the inbound and
outbound edges grouped by type. It lists the tests that lock the result, the relays and register rows that cite it, and
its harvest status on the other seats, and it links to the source lines at the commit.

Lineage is a layered cone, ancestors above and descendants below, built from authored edges only and transitively
reduced. Superseded and retracted ancestors are marked as trust holes, the way dpdgraph marks admitted lemmas; at this
size node-link drawing performs well, as the experiments show. Dense cross-reference blocks, such as arcs against
registers or seats against arcs, are drawn as matrices. Audit views list arcs that nothing depends on, PROVED arcs that
rest on a READING or OPEN result upstream, superseded arcs that are still cited, violations of the sealing order, and
evidence that has gone stale. These are the same classes of fault that LeanArchitect's conversions turned up in
blueprints.

### (b) Boards and dashboards read status, never set it

The top dashboard should state the program's end condition and its progress, as the ETP's did. The record already keeps
such a measure: arcs close with a tally against the 19 free numbers, for example "0 of 19" in W125's status row
(`docs/dossiers/the_weave_2026-10-07/NOTE.md`). Below the tally sit counts by verdict for each seat over time, each
number linked to the query and commit that produced it, since the ETP warns that unverified scripts can mislead
contributors.

A seal board shows each arc's checklist (rule committed, script committed, output committed unread, read out, post hoc
checks labelled), with each step tied to its commit, in the spirit of LIGO's step-by-step memos. A cells dashboard draws
every preregistered cell with its prior and outcome as a grid, like the multiverse plots. A calibration chart of priors
against the rates at which cells held, across the roughly 377 W cells, is a natural and honest measure of the program's
health. Debt boards come straight from the registers: RELAY_LEDGER dispositions (OPEN, ESCALATED after 21 days, BANKED,
DECLINED), main's point register (QUEUED, LEAD, OWNER, DONE, DECLINED), the leads, and the harvest rows. Their columns
use the record's own vocabulary, and each card links to its file and line. If GitHub Projects is used at all, CI should
write to it in one direction only, from the registers, never the reverse. Seats and relays belong first on a static
timeline, which also covers the role of the ETP organiser's daily log. A storyline chart, with one line per seat and
relay sections as the scenes, is worth trying only as an experiment, since no study shows that storylines improve
understanding ([Tanahashi and
Ma](https://ieeevis.org/year/2012/paper/infovis/design-considerations-optimizing-storyline-visualizations)).

### (c) The story: five stages, every thread on one stage

The story should be an interactive slideshow in which every stage is a small martini glass: an author-driven panel
quotes the record's exact claim, status and source, and an exploration panel then opens the same data in the workdesk.
Each stage draws every thread under the enumeration rule its source arc states, printed on screen, and the main mark on
the stage is a joint quantity. Selecting one thread opens a focus panel labelled THREAD VIEW. Analytic panels are
static, in Victor's "across all time" form, and animation is kept to the transitions between stages, which is consistent
with Robertson's results.

| Stage | What the record says | Default (weave) view | Labelled thread focus | Medium |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| First rule | GENESIS's principle (PF) and grammar (GM) rows. Each thread is a closed path of the moves "through the shared fibre, the two records" (`docs/THE_WEAVE.md`) | the rule's text verbatim, pinned to a stated GENESIS version (v1.10 and v1.59 differ); the moves drawn as exact generators acting on a finite stage | none | 2D, exact |
| Threads | each thread is one closed path of the moves; for example, 2,554 words and GENESIS's 758 states to length 12 (`NOTE.md`) | all threads on one shared stage, as the word tree to the stated length or every closed word drawn together, with counts by class | one word's path, with its census name, isosig and a SnapPy link | 2D |
| The weave | the joint action on the shared records; "the three belongs to no single move" (`NOTE.md`) | the shared finite fibre with every move acting at once (a Cayley-style grid of torsion points), with joint quantities as first-class objects | one thread's own action on the fibre | 2D, exact |
| Waves | along the ticks: "at tick n a thread acts on the parities as (φ mod 2)ⁿ" (`NOTE.md`) | a static matrix of ticks against threads covering all 758 states (268 act as involutions, 164 fix all three at every tick) | one thread's row | 2D static; replay optional |
| Towers | each thread's tower of closed fillings. W125 made 641,730 fillings of 42 threads up to Q = 16,000, and labels the towers' finite parts as thread results | every tower on shared, normalised axes, headlined by the weave statement (the same coefficients on all 42 threads) | one tower, or a 3D inside view of one filled manifold | 2D overlay; 3D for single manifolds |

Two naming problems need the owner's ruling before the story is built. The record uses "wave" in two senses: the walk
along the ticks shown above, and the owner's "seed and field" framing of 2026-10-13, which the dossier notes is not the
sense clarified on 2026-10-07. It also uses "tower" both for each thread's tower of fillings and for "the root's tower"
above m004, which belongs to a single thread. The story should cite the definition it uses at each stage.

Three dimensions belong where the object itself is three-dimensional geometry: inside views of filled or cusped
manifolds, and fibres drawn over a base with matching colours, as in Johnson's Hopf pictures. The path is to export the
geometry from SnapPy in CI and render it with three.js, adapting the ray-marching shaders for the Thurston geometries
once their licence has been checked
([code](https://plmlab.math.cnrs.fr/3-dimensional.space/source/-/tree/f63d83aa4b8e633a6907fda7ac2f827d5746dc1f)). The
research notes add, as background they did not source, that closed torus bundles with Anosov monodromy are Sol manifolds
while punctured-torus bundles are hyperbolic; if that holds, the story needs two renderers and a clear label saying
which fibre is meant. A 3D force-directed layout of the record or of the word graph should not appear. Its shapes are
artefacts of the layout algorithm, the reception of the Wolfram project shows the cost of letting such shapes stand in
for computation, and no evidence shows that 3D cures the hairball.

Every frame carries a panel on how it was made, giving the data file and its commit, the script, the projection or
slice, the colour map, what is omitted and the numerical precision. Every frame also passes an automated invariance
check, in which it is rendered under random gauge choices, such as cyclic rotations of words or different fundamental
domains, and the images are compared. Computed content and READING-level interpretation sit on separate layers, and the
reading layer can be switched off. Frames should be generated from data files by templates, with author text written
only once per stage, to avoid Distill's cost per page.

### Weave and thread labels apply to every view

| View | Default scope | Label shown |
|---|---|---|
| Record overview, dashboards, boards | every arc on every seat's branch, at pinned commits | the inclusion rule, in words |
| Story stage | every thread under the source arc's stated enumeration | "ALL THREADS: <rule>, <count>" |
| Arc or W-arc homepage | one arc | the record's own label verbatim, or "unlabelled" |
| Lineage cone, neighbourhood search | one arc's ancestors and descendants | "FOCUS: cone of <id>" |
| Thread homepage, inside view, single tower | one thread | "THREAD VIEW" |
| Gallery of small multiples | several threads, one at a time | "THREAD VIEWS, side by side" |

No default view should open on a single object. The owner's rule names three words that pull the work back to one
thread, "the object", "the family" and "the root", and Hullman and Diakopoulos's account of procedural framing explains
how a default view can pull the same way without a word being said.

### Build it in three releases and judge it by logged insights

Design-study practice favours a small first version, deployed early ([Sedlmair et
al.](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/imager/tr/2012/dsm)), and Saraiya's study found that how well users can select and group
items mattered more than the choice of chart. The first release should hold the cross-seat index, the synthesised W
verdicts, arc homepages, the evolution-matrix overview, search, and the banner showing the build commit. The second
should add cones, audit views, the seal board, the cells and calibration dashboard, and the debt boards. The story comes
third. Each release should be judged in the MILC style ("multi-dimensional in-depth long-term case studies"): over
weeks, with the owner and the seats as the expert users, and with a log of the observations each view produces, coded
for correctness and for whether they led to a new or corrected arc. A view that produces none should be cut.

## Conclusion

What separates a scientific workdesk from an entertaining one is not the medium, since 3D, animation and maps can serve
either purpose. What decides it is whether every mark is derived by code from the record at a pinned commit, links back
to its source line, carries the record's own status and scope label rather than one the interface invents, and survives
an invariance check. The precedents that worked (the ETP, the LMFDB, the Stacks Project, and blueprints once generated
from code) share that discipline; the hairballs, and the Wolfram-style pictures that failed to persuade, lack it. For
origin-axiom the owner's weave rule and the visualisation evidence point the same way. A whole-record overview with a
stated inclusion rule is both the honest default under THE WEAVE and Shneiderman's "overview first" ([Shneiderman
1996](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/5784)), and the LMFDB's completeness flag is a working model for marking a
census over a declared domain apart from a hand-picked subset.

The bottleneck is data, not graphics. The repository already guarantees what most projects lack: citations that resolve,
sealed rules, and lifecycles ordered by commits. What it lacks is one identity for each arc across seats, a verdict
record for the W-arcs, and machine-readable scope labels on most of its results. Building these into the derived index,
not into the record by hand, is the step that makes every later view trustworthy. It is also the point where the front
end could quietly become a second author of claims, if it ever guessed a label the record does not state.
